# Word Choice and Terminology

> Preferred words, terminology rules, and language guidance for GoDaddy product content.

## Actions

Avoid adding unnecessary prepositions. Don't reference a button or its location on the page.

> **Correct:** Click Next to save your changes.
>
> **Incorrect:** Click on Next / Click the Next button / Click the Next button below

For mobile, use bold when guiding the customer to a CTA in copy: "Choose **QR code** for the payment method."

## Adding vs Giving

"Adding" can raise questions about charges or changes. "Giving" frames it as a gift.

- "Hey! I'm giving you a loaf of bread in your bag." — Cool! Thank you!
- "Hey! I'm adding a loaf of bread in your bag." — Why are you doing that?

## Build vs Create

- **Create** — sounds artful and optimistic. Use for what customers do.
- **Build** — sounds more technical. Use for what we do for customers.

> **Rule of thumb:** We build, you create. Also be mindful of overusing "setup."

## Click vs Tap

When writing for mobile, say "tap," not "click."

## Continue vs Next

| Use "Continue" | Use "Next" |
|----------------|-----------|
| Creative, flexible experiences | Technical, procedural processes |
| Open or ongoing flows where something is being created | Step-by-step processes (connection, configuration, setup, billing) |
| Welcoming users back into a flow they left | Often paired with progress bars or steppers |

- "Continue" can also streamline multi-step flows by replacing specific action labels.
- Avoid using "resume" in any instance.

## View vs See

Use "see" instead of "view" for a more modern, human tone — especially in CTAs.

> **Correct:** See Plans
>
> **Incorrect:** View Plans

## Sign In/Out vs Log In/Out

Use "sign in" and "sign out." It's more approachable and modern than "log in" and "log out."

## Setup vs Set Up

- **Set up** (two words) — when it's an action (verb): "Set up your account."
- **Setup** (one word) — when it's a noun or modifying a noun: "It's a 1-click setup process."

## Your vs My

In most instances, use "your" — we are the authoritative voice speaking to the customer.

> Your email account is set up. / Your domain is about to expire.

Don't overuse either "your" or "my." For cards or sections that provide access to products, opt for the name of the tool or feature (e.g., "Devices" or "Dashboard").

**In CTAs:** Try to drop pronouns. If you must, choose "my." Never use "you/your" in CTAs.

## Save On vs Save Off

Don't use both prepositions together. The correct preposition depends on the structure:

> **Correct:** Save on email. / Save 10% on email. / Get 10% off email.
>
> **Incorrect:** Save 10% off on domains.

## New Order

"New Order" is too ambiguous and may confuse customers about discounts applying to renewals.

> **Correct:** Save 30% on your next new item (or product or service)
>
> **Incorrect:** Save 30% on your next new order (or purchase)

## Please

Use "please" only in two cases:

1. Something's not ready and we're asking the user to come back.
2. In error messages.

> We don't have stats yet. Please check back later.
>
> Something's not right. Please try again.

## Oops and Yikes

These words represent an older brand. We're more modern now — avoid things that come off as silly or insincere. See also: Error Messages.

## Free

"Free" speaks for itself — no need to shout it in ALL CAPS or default to an exclamation point. It can come off as overly self-congratulatory.

## GoDaddy

One word. Capital G and capital D: GoDaddy. Don't write in the third person.

> **Correct:** Our web hosting.
>
> **Incorrect:** GoDaddy's web hosting.

## Canceled vs Cancelled

We're minimalists — use the one "l" version: **canceled**.

Exception: "cancellation" (our cancellation policy).

## Traveled vs Travelled

Same as "canceled" — we're minimalists: **traveled**.

## Allowlist and Blocklist

Use "allowlist" and "blocklist." We do not use "whitelist" or "blacklist" due to potential negative connotations.

## Colloquialisms and Idioms

Don't use regional or obscure phrases that might not be easily understood by all users, especially those who don't speak English as a first language.

Avoid phrases like:
- Bite the bullet
- It's not rocket science
- Miss the boat

## Slang

Write in a way everyone can understand, regardless of region or background. Avoid slang, jargon, idioms, vernacular, and any language that can't be easily translated. This creates a voice of inclusion.

## Profanity

For brand, it can work in small doses. For product content, avoid altogether.

## Emojis

The use of emojis is not in line with the GoDaddy visual voice — avoid them in UI content. However, some social media content might contain emojis, so it's safe to use them in sample content.

## Specificity

When you know an exact number, give that number.

> **Correct:** Here are 4 reasons to start a blog.
>
> **Incorrect:** Here are a few reasons to start a blog.

## 2-step Verification

- Include the hyphen: 2-step verification (no caps on "step" or "verification").
- Even in headings (those should be sentence case anyway).
- Shortened form: 2SV.
- Don't write out "two" (aligns with our use of numerals).

## Third-Party Access

How we speak about giving others access depends on the scenario:

- **Connecting to Google/Facebook:** Use "connect" and let their flows take over.
- **Giving employees/partners/subscribers access:** Use "Give Access."

## Localization

We write for English-speaking markets primarily — default to American spelling (color vs colour). Be mindful of products expanding to other markets; for example, headlines translated into German will need to be kept short to fit the space.
